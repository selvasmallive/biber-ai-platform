# T2: corporation income tax

Not a product yet. This records what exists, what it cost to learn, and what
comes next — because T1 is now waiting on data that publishes in late 2026, and
T2 is the work that is not blocked.

## Why T2 is a different shape, not a bigger T1

A T1 is filed for a calendar year, so the year *is* the period. A corporation
picks its own year-end, and its tax year is whatever falls between one and the
next — up to 53 weeks, and frequently not 365 days.

Two consequences run through the whole return:

1. **Amounts prorate.** The business limit behind the small business deduction,
   capital cost allowance and others are reduced in proportion to the days in a
   short tax year.
2. **Rates blend.** A period from 1 July to 30 June sits in two calendar years.
   If a rate changed between them, the tax is apportioned by the days falling
   either side — not by which year the period mostly occupies.

Both reduce to counting days correctly, which is why the foundation is
arithmetic and dates with no tax content at all.

## What exists

### `Period` and `Date` — `freshet-engine/src/period.rs`

Inclusive day counting: 1 January to 31 December is **365 days, not 364**. That
off-by-one would understate every prorated amount in the return by a day,
everywhere, and never fail loudly.

It refuses what cannot be a tax year — a period over 53 weeks is wrong dates
rather than a long year, and 29 February 2025 is not a date.

`proration(denominator)` takes the denominator from the caller rather than the
calendar. The Act prorates the business limit over 365 even in a leap year, so
reading 366 off the calendar would quietly under-claim.

### `PeriodPacks` — `freshet-engine/src/params.rs`

Resolves parameters across a period that straddles a rate change. `blended()`
apportions by days; `at_year_end()` serves the amounts the Act fixes at year-end
instead. It refuses a period whose years it was not given parameters for, rather
than silently using the wrong year's rate.

### `compute_for_period` — `freshet-engine/src/eval.rs`

Injects the period into evaluation as `period.*` facts:

| Fact | |
|---|---|
| `period.days` | inclusive day count |
| `period.is_short`, `period.straddles` | 1 or 0 |
| `period.first_year`, `period.second_year` | calendar years touched |
| `period.days_in_first_year`, `period.days_in_second_year` | the split |

Facts rather than new expression operators, deliberately: proration stays
visible in the form definition. A short-year business limit reads as
`limit × period.days ÷ 365` — arithmetic anyone can check against the Act, with
the 365 written where the Act puts it rather than buried in the engine.

The two year counts are positional. `second_year` is nil for a period inside one
calendar year, so a blend written as `(first × a + second × b) ÷ days` returns
`a` unchanged when there is nothing to blend and needs no special case.

`period.*` is reserved: a caller supplying one is refused, because it would
override the real period with nothing on screen to say so.

**A T1 goes through the same path**, with the period implied by the tax year. A
test asserts the two entry points agree — if they ever diverge, every T1 golden
vector is suspect.

### The core return — `data/t2-federal.json`, `data/t2-on.json`

The federal Part I flow: net income, taxable income, the base 38% amount, the
provincial abatement, the small business deduction and the general rate
reduction. Twelve federal lines and five Ontario ones.

**The federal/provincial contract carried over unchanged.** That was the open
question, and the answer is that the split was not shaped around personal tax:
the federal T2 names no province, Ontario supplies `prov.tax`, and
`FormSet::compose` joins them exactly as for a T1. A test asserts it rather than
the comment claiming it.

Ontario corporate tax is two rates on two kinds of income rather than a bracket
table — corporate tax turns on how income was earned, not how much of it there
is. A bracket table would have been the wrong shape entirely, which is worth
knowing before the other twelve provinces are written.

### Where proration bites

The business limit is `limit × period.days ÷ 365`, and the small business
deduction follows it down. A corporation incorporated in September gets 108/365
of the limit, a smaller deduction, and more tax on identical income. Tests
assert the ratio, which is checkable even though the limit itself is a
placeholder.

The 365 is written in the form rather than derived from the calendar because
ITA 125(5) says 365 even in a leap year. Reading 366 off the calendar would
over-claim.

### Rate blending — wired in

A fiscal year crossing a calendar boundary is now taxed at each year's rates in
proportion to the days falling in it.

The form says which parameters blend, via `blended_param`. Blending everything
automatically would have been wrong: the Act fixes some amounts for the year
rather than setting a rate in force on a given day, and the business limit is
one of them. Which of the two a parameter is, is a tax question, so it belongs
in the form where it can be read against the legislation rather than in the
engine where it cannot.

Three properties, each with a test:

- A rate that moved lands **between** the two years, weighted by days.
- A rate that did **not** move produces exactly what a full year inside either
  produces — apportioning must not move an answer that has nothing to move.
- A plain `param` still resolves from the **closing** year, which is what the
  year-end amounts need.

`compute_for_period` now **refuses** a straddling period given one year of
parameters, rather than quietly applying the closing year's rate to the whole
period. That is the failure this feature exists to prevent, so it fails loudly
instead of being computed wrongly.

The unverified set is the **union** across both years. A return drawing on two
years is only as trustworthy as the worse of them, and must not be filable
because one of them happens to be clean.

## What is next, in order

1. **Schedule 1**, the reconciliation from accounting profit to net income for
   tax purposes. Currently a single entered figure.
3. **GIFI.** Financial statement information — large, mechanical, and the thing
   that makes the return actually filable.
4. **The remaining provinces**, now known to be cheap: a supplement plus two
   rates.
5. **Corporate tax data**, under the same discipline as T1: every parameter
   cited, nothing marked verified without a primary source.

## What is deliberately not done

Every corporate rate is an unverified placeholder, pinned in the verification
ledger like the provincial ones. No T2 return can be filed, and a test asserts
it. The shape is the deliverable; the numbers are not.

Rate blending is done. Which parameters blend is itself unverified: the four
federal rates are marked as blending and the business limit is not, on the
reading that a rate is in force on a day while a limit is fixed for a year. That
reading needs confirming against the T2 guide before any corporate rate is
marked verified — a correct rate applied under the wrong apportionment rule is
still a wrong return.

## One correction worth keeping

The multiply-before-divide ordering in `blended()` was originally justified with
a claim that the alternative drifts, and a test was written asserting it. The
test failed: the orderings agree. A Python model found 96 disagreeing cases, the
test was rewritten around one of them, and it failed again — the model did not
represent `rust_decimal`.

Having been wrong twice, the comment and the test now assert only what holds: an
unchanged rate survives any day split exactly, and the ordering is a precaution
rather than a fix for an observed defect. Asserting a difference that does not
exist would have been worse than asserting nothing.
